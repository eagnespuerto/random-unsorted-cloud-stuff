The Unknown
UV Absorber

Something in Venus's upper cloud deck soaks up near-ultraviolet and blue sunlight, painting the otherwise bland yellow-white planet with dark, fast-moving streaks. Nearly a century after it was first photographed, nobody knows for sure what it is.

01What is "the absorber"?

Venus is wrapped in a ~20 km-thick deck of concentrated sulfuric-acid droplets (roughly 48–70 km altitude). Pure H2SO4 droplets should look almost perfectly white. Instead, the cloud tops absorb strongly between about 320 and 500 nm. Below ~320 nm the absorption is well explained by sulfur dioxide gas. The longer-wavelength part comes from an unidentified substance: the unknown UV absorber.

Why it matters

The absorber takes in roughly half of all solar energy Venus absorbs. That heat, deposited high in the clouds, is thought to help drive the atmosphere's super-rotation. Its patchiness also produces the dark Y- and C-shaped markings that let astronomers track the winds at all.

It's also variable. Akatsuki and Venus Express data showed that the planet's 365 nm albedo changed by up to a factor of two between 2006 and 2017, and those changes tracked changes in wind speed (Lee et al. 2019).

Absorption, schematic

Shapes are illustrative, not measured data. The absorber band is broad, peaks in the near-UV, and tails off into the blue, which is part of why Venus looks yellowish.

02Leading theories

A good candidate has to match the spectrum's shape, sit at the right altitude (mostly ~57–70 km), survive in hot concentrated sulfuric acid, and be abundant enough. So far no single candidate has done all four.

Ferric chloride

FeCl₃ · dissolved in H₂SO₄ droplets

Iron chloride, possibly lofted from the surface or from meteoritic dust, would absorb in the right UV range. Venera and Vega X-ray fluorescence data suggested iron and chlorine in cloud particles.

ForGood spectral match near 360 nm; Fe and Cl both detected.
AgainstUnclear how it stays stable in acid; doesn't explain all of the absorption by itself.
Zasova et al. 1981 · Krasnopolsky 1985, 2017 · Pérez-Hoyos et al. 2018

Polysulfur (Sn)

S₃, S₄ … S₈ · elemental sulfur allotropes

Photochemistry of SO₂ and OCS can produce short sulfur chains that absorb in the near-UV and blue. Elemental sulfur is a natural by-product of Venus's sulfur cycle.

ForChemically expected; S₃/S₄ absorb at visible and near-UV wavelengths.
AgainstHard to keep enough of it at the cloud tops; spectral fit is imperfect.
Hapke & Nelson 1975 · Toon et al. 1982 · Mills et al. 2007

Disulfur dioxide

cis/trans-OSSO · (SO)₂

Quantum-chemistry calculations showed OSSO isomers absorb strongly at 320–400 nm. Formed from SO photochemistry, they could account for much of the near-UV absorption.

ForStrong absorption in the right band; forms naturally from SO.
AgainstA gas, so hard to square with absorber linked to particles; predicted amounts are uncertain.
Frandsen, Wallace & Kjaergaard 2016 · Wu et al. 2018

Iron sulfate minerals

rhomboclase · acid ferric sulfate

Lab work found these minerals are stable in concentrated sulfuric acid, unlike FeCl₃. Their combined absorbance matched Venus's UV and blue absorption features well.

ForStable in Venus-like acid; good fit at several wavelengths.
AgainstDoesn't fully explain the 350 nm region; iron source and supply rate unknown.
Jiang et al. 2024 (Science Advances)

Cloud-dwelling microbes

Sagan & Morowitz 1967

The cloud layer at ~50–60 km has Earth-like temperatures (~0–60 °C) and pressures around 1 bar. Sagan and Morowitz argued this could be a habitable zone for floating organisms, decades before the absorber became a focus.

ForOnly Earth-like temperature/pressure zone in the solar system off Earth.
AgainstExtreme acidity and very little water; no known life copes with this.
Sagan & Morowitz 1967 (Nature) · Grinspoon 1997

Microbial "sunscreen"

S₈ as UV shield + energy converter

Hypothesized organisms might use cyclo-octasulfur (S₈) to block harmful UV and turn it into usable light. The patchy absorber would then partly be a signature of the organisms themselves.

ForTies absorber, sulfur chemistry and energy budget together.
AgainstPurely speculative; no organisms or biomarkers found.
Schulze-Makuch & Irwin 2002, 2004

Spectral & size coincidences

Limaye et al. 2018 (Astrobiology)

Noted that the absorber's spectrum resembles some light-absorbing terrestrial microbes, and that the large "mode 3" particles seen by Pioneer Venus are similar in size to bacteria.

ForProposes concrete, testable measurements for future probes.
AgainstSimilar spectra don't prove anything; mineral explanations also fit.
Limaye et al. 2018 · Knollenberg & Hunten 1980 (mode 3)

Phosphine controversy

PH₃ at ~1–20 ppb (disputed)

A 2020 report of phosphine reignited interest in cloud biology. It's not a claim about the absorber, but it focused new attention on unexplained chemistry in the cloud deck.

ForReanalyses keep a weak signal open; it spurred new missions.
AgainstStrongly contested; data calibration and SO₂ confusion are disputed.
Greaves et al. 2020 · Villanueva et al. 2021 · Snellen et al. 2020
Status check: No one has directly detected life on Venus. Most researchers favor chemical explanations, possibly several absorbers working together. A definitive answer will probably require in-situ measurements inside the clouds.

03Discovery & observation

1927

Ross photographs the markings

Frank E. Ross at Mount Wilson Observatory photographs Venus through ultraviolet filters and records dark, banded features that are invisible to the eye. This is the first evidence of the absorber.

1957

The four-day rotation

Amateur astronomer Charles Boyer, working from Brazzaville, notices UV features repeating about every 4 days. With Henri Camichel at Pic du Midi, he confirms it by 1961. This was the first hint of super-rotation.

1962–64

Radar finds the slow, backwards spin

Radar from Goldstone and Arecibo (Goldstein, Carpenter and others) shows the solid planet rotates retrograde in about 243 days. The clouds are therefore moving ~60× faster than the ground beneath them.

1967

Life in the clouds?

Carl Sagan and Harold Morowitz propose that the temperate cloud layer could host life.

1972–73

Sulfuric-acid clouds identified

Godfrey Sill and Louise & Andrew Young show the clouds are concentrated H₂SO₄ droplets. Since those droplets should be white, an extra absorber must be present.

1974

Mariner 10 flyby

First spacecraft UV images show the planet-wide "Y" feature and the detailed cloud structure in motion.

1978–92

Pioneer Venus

The orbiter's photopolarimeter collected 14 years of UV imaging. The Large Probe's particle spectrometer measured cloud particle size modes, including the debated large "mode 3" particles.

1985

Vega balloons & descent probes

The Soviet Vega 1 and 2 balloons drifted through the middle cloud deck to measure winds. Their descent landers' UV spectrometers profiled absorption through the clouds.

2006–14

Venus Express

ESA's Venus Monitoring Camera tracked UV features for eight years and found cloud-top winds speeding up from ~85 to ~110 m/s. It also found absorber contrast that varied over time.

2015–24

Akatsuki

JAXA's orbiter imaged at 283 nm (SO₂) and 365 nm (unknown absorber). It found a factor-of-two albedo change linked to the winds and to Venus's energy balance. The mission ended in 2024 after contact was lost.

2020

Phosphine claim

A disputed phosphine detection renewed debate over cloud chemistry and possible biology.

2024

Iron sulfates in the lab

Lab measurements show rhomboclase and acid ferric sulfate are stable in Venus-like acid and match much of the absorption.

2030s

Next wave

NASA's DAVINCI probe is designed to descend through the clouds and measure their composition along the way. VERITAS and ESA's EnVision will map the planet from orbit, and smaller privately funded probes have been proposed to sample cloud particles directly.
